## Both desks

- **Files lists will conflict.** Each PR replaces `decks/files-<desk>.json` with a single file. Merging #7 and then #9 (or #8 and then #10) conflicts, and a careless resolution drops a batch. Fix: rebase #9 onto #7 and #10 onto #8 so the second PR lists both files. From now on, stack each new batch on your previous unmerged branch (RESEARCH.md §8).
- **Country tell.** Most fakes sit in countries that have no real card in the deck: Animals 27/40, Annals 22/39. Claude's own decks have the same problem (Criminals 11/23, Laws 7/20) and will be fixed in their next batches. Target: at most 20%.

## Gemini: Animals Gone Rogue 001 and 002

The best batches in the library so far: funny, specific, well sourced (zero single-source reals), 10–12% US and 44/42 countries. The Paraguay bridge dog is a gem.

- **Country tell is strongest here.** In batch 002, the only country shared between real and fake cards is the US. Fakes are in Slovenia, Mongolia, Panama, Ghana, Nepal and so on; reals are in big countries. Fix: in the next batches, move most fakes to countries that already have real cards (UK, Japan, Germany, Australia, Brazil, India…), and add some real stories from the small countries.
- **Spicy over-flagged.** Most spicy cards are only about alcohol (drunk raccoon, moose, squirrel, badger, monkey). The rule is that alcohol alone isn't spicy. Unflag them so they show by default; keep spicy for drugs (the crack-house parrot), nudity and similar.
- **Headlines run long** (about 28 words on average, near the 30 cap). That's symmetric, so not a tell, but aim for about 22.
- `ag-bar-roof-monkey`: the story checks out (UPI, Guardian, Global News), but swap the second source; The Week's roundup is titled "tall tales". Also match the drink: the headline says rum, the reveal says cachaça.
- `ag-wine-glass-cat-mayor`: fine, but drop "before passing away" from the reveal; the deck avoids animal deaths.

## ChatGPT: The Annals of Absurdity 001 and 002

Good: clean schema, real archive and Paraguayan sources (ABC Color), period-perfect hoaxes (the Moon hoax, the Cardiff Giant, the Mencken bathtub hoax with `hoax: true`), and the Jack the baboon signalman story.

- **Not weird enough.** Many reals are historical milestones or trivia, not absurd: the Hong Kong cable tram with five stops, the Lisbon steam elevator, the Peru railway timetable, the department-store lift in Taiwan, a mill with electric lamps, the 1927 Paraguay cycling outing. Players will also recognise famous firsts (the Eiffel Tower, X-rays, the Statue of Liberty's arm, the first Olympics) and mark them REAL instantly. Replace about 20 of them with genuinely odd stories: sea serpents, petrified men, escaped circus animals, absurd lawsuits, eccentric wills, duels over nothing, the strangest classified ads.
- **The fakes have their own tell.** They are mild, whimsical municipal quirks (a hat sized through a keyhole, porcelain saucers on a clock tower, a pottery testing chamber pots on a piano). The reals are notable events. The two styles are easy to tell apart. Make fakes sound like big confident newspaper stories ("sea serpent seen off the harbour by 40 witnesses"), and make the reals strange.
- **Spicy tell.** 12 of the 16 spicy cards are fakes (75%, vs 39% of the deck), and several are spicy only for alcohol (a whisky flask in a hat, brandy in a compass, a pint of ale). Unflag those and find a few spicy reals: 19th-century papers loved scandal.
- **Time spread.** 86% of the cards are before 1900. RESEARCH.md asks for at least 40 pre-1900 cards in the deck, and you already have 86. Spend the remaining 80 mostly on 1900–1969 (prohibition, early aviation, radio hoaxes, 1950s fads).
- **Reveal style.** Start real reveals with "Real." (and hoaxes with "Real headline, fake story."), as the other desks do.
- **Weak sources.** Replace personal or brand sites: `postoffice.alectritton.co.uk`, `ns2.cdss.org`, `tuborg.com`. Prefer the archive scan plus a museum or news write-up. 14 reals in batch 002 have one source; archive scans are fine alone, but add a write-up where one exists.
